# General Aptitude

**General Aptitude (GA)** is the "common sense" part of the GATE paper. It checks skills you use every day. Can you read a short English sentence and spot the right word? Can you work out a discount in a shop? Can you read a cricket score chart? Can you picture how a dice looks from another side? That is all GA is.

Think of GA like the **warm-up overs** in a cricket match. The bowling is easy, and every run counts the same as runs scored later. Many students ignore it and lose easy marks.

**Why it matters for data science and AI.** A data scientist reads charts, works with percentages and averages, and explains results in clear English. GA trains exactly these habits. Spatial questions also train you to picture shapes, which helps with vectors and matrices.

**How GATE tests it.** Every GATE paper has the same GA section: **10 questions, 15 marks**. Q.1 to Q.5 carry 1 mark each. Q.6 to Q.10 carry 2 marks each. From 2024 to 2026, every GA question in the DA paper was an **MCQ** (multiple choice question: pick one of four options). A wrong answer costs one third of its marks: $-\frac{1}{3}$ for a 1-mark question and $-\frac{2}{3}$ for a 2-mark question.

In the 30 past questions (2024 to 2026), the split was:

| Topic | Questions | Marks |
|---|---|---|
| Quantitative Aptitude | 11 | 17 |
| Verbal Ability | 8 | 11 |
| Spatial / Visual Reasoning | 6 | 9 |
| Data Interpretation | 3 | 4 |
| Analytical / Logical Reasoning | 2 | 4 |

**Study order, and why.**
1. **Verbal Ability** first. The rules are short, and you get quick wins.
2. **Quantitative Aptitude** next. It carries the most marks.
3. **Data Interpretation**. It reuses percentages and averages from Quant.
4. **Analytical / Logical Reasoning**. It needs calm, step-by-step thinking.
5. **Spatial / Visual Reasoning** last. It needs practice with figures.

Spend about 30 minutes a week on GA. Solve the GA sections of past GATE papers from **all branches** (CS, EE, ME, and so on). The GA section is the same style in every branch, so this gives you hundreds of extra practice questions.

**Free resources:**
- Past GATE papers and answer keys on the official GATE website (all branches).
- R. S. Aggarwal, *Quantitative Aptitude for Competitive Examinations* and *A Modern Approach to Verbal and Non-Verbal Reasoning* (any library copy works).
- Wren and Martin, *High School English Grammar and Composition* (for tenses, prepositions and articles).

## Verbal Ability

### The big idea
Verbal Ability tests simple English. You must pick the right word, the right tense, or the right meaning. Each question hides **one rule**. Find that rule, and the answer is easy.

### Before you start
This topic needs only a few school grammar ideas. Each one is explained here from zero.

**1. What a verb is.** A verb is the "action" or "being" word in a sentence. In "Ravi **plays** cricket", the verb is "plays". In "She **is** tired", the verb is "is".

**2. The three forms of a verb (V1, V2, V3).** Most English verbs have three main forms. V1 is the base form (go, eat, start). V2 is the simple past (went, ate, started). V3 is the past participle (gone, eaten, started), and it is used after "have / has / had".

**3. Helping verbs.** Some verbs help another verb to show time. The three big helpers are **be** (am, is, are, was, were, been), **have** (have, has, had) and **will / would**. *Example:* in "She **has** eaten", "has" is the helper and "eaten" is the main verb.

**4. Subject.** The subject is the person or thing doing the action. In "The boys **are** playing", the subject is "the boys". The verb must match the subject in number (one or many).

**5. A timeline.** Draw a line from left (past) to right (future), with "now" in the middle. Every tense is just a way to place an action on this line. We will use this picture to explain every tense rule below.

**6. Vowel and consonant sounds.** The vowel letters are a, e, i, o, u. But what matters in speech is the **sound**, not the letter. "Hour" starts with the letter h but the sound "aw", which is a vowel sound.

### Intuition
Imagine you send a WhatsApp message to your friend: "I **had been waiting** at the station for an hour when the train came." Your friend understands two things. First, the waiting started earlier. Second, the waiting was still going on when the train arrived.

The tense carries this timeline. GATE questions work like this too. They give a sentence with a gap, and the rest of the sentence gives clues: a time word ("when", "since", "for"), a past event ("it started to rain"), or a meaning ("this house is in a mess").

> **Intuition:** Treat every verbal question like a detective case. Look for the clue words first. Then ask: "Which rule does this clue point to?" Only then look at the options.

For **analogies**, think of a family tree. "Father : Son" is a relation. "Mother : Daughter" is the same relation. GATE gives you one pair and asks you to build a second pair with the same link.

For **reading passages**, think of a judge in a court. The judge decides only from the evidence given. You must also answer only from what the passage says, not from what you know outside.

### Words you need
- **Tense**: the form of a verb that shows time. *Example:* "play" (now), "played" (past), "will play" (future).
- **Past participle (V3)**: the third form of a verb, used after "have/has/had". *Example:* go, went, **gone**.
- **Continuous**: an action that goes on for some time, made with "be + verb-ing". *Example:* "She **is reading**."
- **Perfect**: a tense made with "have/has/had + V3". It shows an action that is **complete before** some point of time. *Example:* "I **have finished**."
- **Analogy**: two pairs of words with the same relation. *Example:* Hot : Cold :: Up : Down (both pairs are opposites).
- **Synonym**: a word with the same meaning. *Example:* big and large.
- **Antonym**: a word with the opposite meaning. *Example:* rich and poor.
- **Preposition**: a small word that shows place, time or a link. *Example:* at, in, on, of, for.
- **Phrasal verb**: a verb plus a small word that together mean something new. *Example:* "put off" means postpone.
- **Article**: the words "a", "an", "the". *Example:* "**an** apple", "**a** car".
- **Inference**: a conclusion that must follow from the given text. *Example:* "All shops close on Sunday. Today is Sunday." Inference: this shop is closed today.
- **Conditional**: an "if" sentence. *Example:* "If it rains, I will stay home."

### Formulas, explained
In English, the "formulas" are grammar patterns. Learn these five.

**1. Past perfect continuous**

$$\text{had} + \text{been} + \text{verb-ing}$$

- **Read it as:** "had been doing something".
- **Symbols:** "verb-ing" means the verb with "-ing", like playing or cooking.
- **Why it makes sense:** "had" pushes the time into the past. "been ...-ing" shows the action was still going on. So the action was running up to a past moment.
- **Use it when:** the sentence has a simple past event, like "when it started to rain", and the gap describes what was going on before it.

**2. Past perfect**

$$\text{had} + \text{V3}$$

- **Read it as:** "had done something".
- **Symbols:** V3 is the past participle (gone, eaten, started).
- **Why it makes sense:** Of two past actions, the one that finished first takes "had".
- **Use it when:** you see "before", "after" or "by the time" with two past actions. *Example:* "The film **had started** before we arrived."

**3. Third conditional (imaginary past)**

$$\text{If} + \text{had} + \text{V3}, \;\; \text{would have} + \text{V3}$$

- **Read it as:** "If X had happened, Y would have happened."
- **Symbols:** X is the "if" part. Y is the result part.
- **Why it makes sense:** We use this pattern to dream about a past that we cannot change. So it secretly tells you that **X did not happen**, and so Y did not happen.
- **Use it when:** the question asks what is "true" from an "if ... had ..." sentence.

**4. Prepositions of place and time**

| Word | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| at | an exact point | at the door, at 5 pm |
| in | inside, or a longer period | in the room, in June |
| on | a surface, or a day | on the table, on Monday |
| of | belongs to | the door of the cabin |
| for | how long | for two hours, for the night |
| since | starting point | since 2020 |

- **Read it as:** "at" for a point, "on" for a surface or day, "in" for inside or a long period.
- **Why it makes sense:** Think of size. "at" is a dot. "on" is a flat surface. "in" is a box around you.
- **Use it when:** a gap sits before a place or a time.

**5. Subject-verb agreement**

$$\text{singular subject} \rightarrow \text{singular verb}$$

- **Read it as:** "one thing takes 'is/has'; many things take 'are/have'."
- **Rules:** "One of the boys **is**." "Each of them **is**." "The team **has**." With "either A or B", the verb matches B (the nearer noun).
- **Why it makes sense:** In "one of the boys", the real subject is "one", not "boys".
- **Use it when:** the gap is a verb (is/are, has/have) after a tricky subject.

**Other quick rules:**
- **Articles:** use "an" before a vowel **sound**, not a vowel letter. "an hour" (the h is silent), "a university" (it sounds like "you").
- **Phrasal verbs** seen in GATE: **clear up** = tidy, or explain; **take up with** = raise a complaint with someone; **bring up** = mention a topic; **look up** = search for (in a dictionary). Also learn: put off = postpone, call off = cancel, put up with = tolerate, turn down = reject.
- **Analogy method:** (1) Say the relation of the first pair in one short phrase. (2) Use the same phrase on the second pair. (3) Reject words that only **sound** similar.

> **Remember:** In "If + had + V3", the "if" part did NOT happen. In "If + past, would + verb" ("If I were rich, I would travel"), the "if" part is not true now.

### Where the formulas come from
Grammar rules are not random. Each tense is built from small **Lego blocks**, and each block always adds the same meaning. Once you know the blocks, you can build every tense yourself.

**A. The three Lego blocks of English tenses**

1. **Block "be + verb-ing" = still going on.** "She is reading" means the reading has started and not yet stopped. Because the "-ing" form always describes an action in progress, any tense that contains "be + -ing" is called **continuous**.
2. **Block "have + V3" = finished before a reference point.** "I have eaten" means the eating is complete, and I am looking back at it from now. Because "have + V3" always looks back at a completed action, any tense with it is called **perfect**.
3. **The helper's own time tells WHEN the reference point is.** "have / has" puts the reference point at now. "had" (the past of "have") puts it at a past moment. "will have" puts it in the future.

So every tense is: (time of the helper) + (perfect block or not) + (continuous block or not). The table below shows the "had" family, which GATE loves.

| Built from | Tense | Meaning on the timeline |
|---|---|---|
| had + V3 | past perfect | finished before a past moment |
| had + been + verb-ing | past perfect continuous | going on up to a past moment |
| have + been + verb-ing | present perfect continuous | going on up to now |

**B. Why "had been playing" (formula 1)**

Take the GATE 2025 sentence: "We ___ tennis for an hour when it suddenly started to rain."

1. Find the reference moment: "it started to rain" is simple past. So the reference moment is a past moment, not now.
2. A past reference moment needs the helper "had", because "had" is the past form of "have" (block 3).
3. The tennis lasted "for an hour" before that moment, so it was complete up to that point: perfect block, "had + V3". The V3 of "be" is "been", which gives "had been".
4. The tennis was still in progress when the rain came, so we add the continuous block "-ing": "had been playing".

**Result:** had + been + playing. Each word is there for one reason: "had" = past reference point, "been" = perfect, "-ing" = still going on.

**C. Why "had + V3" marks the earlier action (formula 2)**

1. Take two past events: "We arrived" and "The film started". Both are in the past.
2. If we say "The film started when we arrived", the two events happen at the same moment.
3. To show the film started **earlier**, we use the perfect block with a past helper: "The film **had started** when we arrived." This is allowed because "had + V3" means "already complete at a past reference moment" (block 2 + block 3).

**Result:** "had" is a "one step further back" marker. It is formula 1 without the "-ing" block.

**D. Why the third conditional means "it did NOT happen" (formula 3)**

English uses a trick: to talk about something **unreal**, it moves the verb **one step back in time**. This is called back-shifting.

1. A real future possibility: "If it **rains**, I **will** stay home." (present + will). It may really happen.
2. An unreal present: "If I **were** rich, I **would** travel." The verb moved back from "am" to "were", and "will" moved back to "would". This back-step signals "this is not true now".
3. An unreal past: start from a past fact, "The bridge **was** not repaired." To imagine the opposite, move one more step back: "was repaired" becomes "**had been** repaired", and "would" gains "have": "the bus **would have** reached on time".

**Result:** the pattern "If + had + V3, would have + V3" is built by stepping back past the real past. You only step back past reality when you are imagining something that did not happen. That is why it always means the opposite really happened.

**E. Why "at / on / in" follow size (formula 4)**

1. "at" goes with things we treat as a **point**: "at the door", "at 5 pm". A clock time has no length, so it is a point.
2. "on" goes with a **surface or a single day**: "on the table", "on Monday". A day is longer than a point but is one flat block on a calendar.
3. "in" goes with something that **surrounds** you: "in the room", "in June", "in 2020". A month or a year is a big container of days.
4. "for" measures a **length** of time ("for two hours"), while "since" names the **starting point** ("since 2019"). So "for" answers "how long?" and "since" answers "from when?".

**F. Why the verb follows the real subject (formula 5)**

1. In "One of the boys ___ late", ask "who is late?" The answer is "one", a single person.
2. "of the boys" only tells us **which group** the one comes from. It is a describing phrase, not the subject.
3. One person takes the singular verb, so: "One of the boys **is** late."
4. With "either A or B", only one of them is chosen, so the verb agrees with the one nearest to it.

**G. Why "an" before a vowel sound**

1. Say "a apple" aloud quickly. Your voice has to stop between the two vowel sounds.
2. Adding "n" joins the sounds smoothly: "an apple". That is the only purpose of the "n".
3. Because the rule is about the voice, it follows the **sound**: "an hour" (sound "aw"), but "a university" (sound "yoo", which starts like a consonant).

### Examples
**Example 1 (easy):** "She has lived in Pune ___ 2019." (A) for (B) since (C) from (D) at

1. 2019 is a **starting point**, not a length of time.
2. A starting point takes "since" (rule E, step 4).
3. "for" would need a length, like "for five years".

**Result:** (B) since.

**Example 2 (medium):** "By the time the guests arrived, she ___ for three hours." (A) cooks (B) has been cooking (C) had been cooking (D) was cooked

1. "the guests arrived" is simple past. That is our past reference moment.
2. The cooking went on for three hours **up to** that moment. That is pattern 1: had + been + verb-ing.
3. (B) links to the present (helper "has"), so it is wrong. (D) is passive: it means she herself was cooked.

**Result:** (C) had been cooking.

**Example 3 (GATE level):** Frugal : Stingy :: Confident : ___ (A) Shy (B) Arrogant (C) Humble (D) Brave

1. **Frugal** means careful with money (a good quality). **Stingy** means too careful with money (a fault).
2. The relation is "a good quality taken too far becomes a fault".
3. **Confident** taken too far becomes **Arrogant** (too proud).
4. Shy and Humble are near-opposites of confident. Brave is a different quality.

**Result:** (B) Arrogant.

**Example 4 (GATE level):** "If the bridge had been repaired, the bus would have reached on time." Which statement is true?

1. The pattern is "If + had + V3, would have + V3", the third conditional.
2. This pattern is used only to imagine an unreal past (rule D).
3. So the real past is the opposite of both parts.

**Result:** The bridge was **not** repaired, and the bus did **not** reach on time.

### Common mistakes
> **Common mistake:** Choosing "have been playing" when the other half of the sentence is in the past ("it started to rain"). The present perfect connects to **now**. A past moment needs "had been playing".

> **Common mistake:** Reading "If it had been a success" as "it was a success". The third conditional always means the opposite really happened.

> **Common mistake:** Picking a lookalike word in an analogy, like "yelling" for "yearning" or "vein" for "vain". Always check the **meaning**, not the spelling.

> **Common mistake:** In inference questions, choosing an option with strong words like "all", "no", "always" or "essential". The passage rarely supports such a big claim.

> **Common mistake:** Using "a" before every word that starts with a consonant letter. It is "an hour" and "an MBA", because the **sound** starts with a vowel.

### How GATE asks it
- **2024 Q.1:** analogy by rising strength: sick, infirm, moribund; so silly, **vain**, daft. Traps: lookalikes vein and fawn.
- **2025 Q.1:** synonym analogy: courage : bravery :: yearning : **longing**. Traps: yelling, yawning.
- **2026 Q.1:** antonym analogy: verbosity : brevity :: insolence : **respect**.
- **2025 Q.2:** tense: "We **had been playing** tennis ... when it suddenly started to rain."
- **2026 Q.5:** third conditional: "If his latest movie had been a commercial success..." means the movie was **not** a success.
- **2026 Q.6 (2 marks):** three prepositions in one sentence: parted **at** the door **of** the cabin rented **for** the night.
- **2025 Q.6 (2 marks):** match four phrasal verbs (clear up, take up, bring up, look up) to four situations.
- **2024 Q.6 (2 marks):** inference from a short passage about a gene mutation and milk. Reject the "all", "no" and "essential" options.

> **GATE tip:** Q.1 was an analogy in every year from 2024 to 2026. For multi-blank questions, fill the blank you are surest about first. Then cross out every option that does not match it.

### Summary
- First, every verbal question hides one rule, and clue words ("when", "since", "if ... had") point to it.
- Tenses are built from three blocks: "be + -ing" means still going on, "have + V3" means finished before a reference point, and the helper's time (have / had / will) places that reference point.
- So "had been playing" means "going on up to a past moment", and "had + V3" means "the earlier of two past actions".
- English shows an unreal idea by stepping the verb back in time. That is why "If + had + V3" always means the opposite really happened.
- Prepositions follow size (at = point, on = surface or day, in = container), and the verb agrees with the real subject, not the nearest noun.
- For analogies and passages, name the relation or stick to the evidence, and always check meaning, not spelling.
- Next topic builds on this: the same careful reading of words like "at most", "at least" and "must" decides answers in Logical Reasoning and Data Interpretation.

### Quick self-check
1. "I ___ for the bus for 20 minutes when it finally came." Fill the gap.
**Answer:** had been waiting (an action going on up to a past moment).

2. "If she had studied, she would have passed." Did she pass?
**Answer:** No. The third conditional means she did not study and did not pass.

3. Complete: Ancient : Modern :: Generous : ___
**Answer:** Mean (or Stingy). The relation is "opposites".

## Quantitative Aptitude

### The big idea
Quantitative Aptitude is school maths done fast: percentages, ratios, powers, logs, series, counting, averages and a little geometry. Most GATE questions need **one smart observation**, not long calculation.

### Before you start
These school ideas are used again and again below. Each one is explained from zero.

**1. Fractions and decimals.** A fraction $\frac{a}{b}$ means "a parts out of b equal parts". *Example:* $\frac{3}{4}$ of a pizza is 3 slices of a pizza cut into 4. As a decimal, $\frac34 = 0.75$.

**2. Variables and equations.** A letter like $x$ stands for an unknown number. An equation like $x + 3 = 5$ says both sides are equal, so $x = 2$. You may do the same thing to both sides (add, multiply, take log) and they stay equal.

**3. Powers.** $a^n$ means $a$ multiplied by itself $n$ times. *Example:* $3^4 = 3 \times 3 \times 3 \times 3 = 81$. Two special cases: $a^1 = a$ and $a^0 = 1$ (explained in "Where the formulas come from").

**4. Logarithm (log).** A log answers the question "what power do I need?". $\log_{10} 1000 = 3$ because $10^3 = 1000$. When no base is written, GATE usually means base 10, and every log in one question uses the same base.

**5. Sum symbol $\sum$.** The Greek capital letter sigma means "add them all up". *Example:* $\sum_{i=1}^{3} i = 1 + 2 + 3 = 6$.

**6. Factorial $n!$.** Multiply all whole numbers from $n$ down to 1. *Example:* $3! = 3 \times 2 \times 1 = 6$. By agreement, $0! = 1$.

**7. Angles and sine.** The three angles of any triangle add to $180^\circ$. In a right-angled triangle, $\sin$ of an angle $=$ (side opposite the angle) $\div$ (longest side). Values to know: $\sin 30^\circ = \frac12$, $\sin 60^\circ = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}$, $\sin 90^\circ = 1$, and $\sin(180^\circ - \theta) = \sin\theta$.

**8. Basic probability.** Probability $=$ (number of favourable outcomes) $\div$ (number of all equally likely outcomes). It is always between 0 and 1. If two events do not affect each other (they are **independent**), the chance of both is the product of their chances.

### Intuition
Think of a shopkeeper in a market. He never takes out a calculator. A shirt costs 500 rupees with a 20% discount? He says "400" in one second.

Why? He thinks "20% off means I pay 80%, and 80% of 500 is 400". He uses a **shortcut picture**, not a formula sheet.

GATE Quant rewards the same habit. Look at 2025 Q.9. It asks for $\frac{2^{x^2}}{2^{2x}}$.

A slow student finds $x$ first. A smart student notices this equals $2^{x^2-2x}$, and the given equation already tells you $x^2 - 2x = 3$. So the answer is $2^3$, with no need to find $x$.

> **Intuition:** Before you calculate, ask: "What exactly is being asked? Can I get it directly?" GATE often asks for an expression, not for $x$ itself.

Here is a second picture. A **series** is like saving money every day: 10 rupees, then 20, then 30. A **geometric series** is like cutting a cake in half again and again: $\frac12$, then $\frac14$, then $\frac18$.

Even if you cut forever, the pieces add up to exactly one whole cake. That is why an infinite series can have a finite sum.

### Words you need
- **Percentage**: "out of 100". *Example:* 25% means 25 out of 100, which is $\frac14$.
- **Ratio**: a comparison of two amounts by division. *Example:* 6 boys and 4 girls give boys : girls = 6 : 4 = 3 : 2.
- **Exponent (power)**: how many times a number is multiplied by itself. *Example:* $2^3 = 2 \times 2 \times 2 = 8$. Here 2 is the **base** and 3 is the exponent.
- **Logarithm (log)**: the opposite of a power. It asks "what power gives this number?" *Example:* $\log_2 8 = 3$ because $2^3 = 8$.
- **AP (arithmetic progression)**: a list where you add the same number each time. *Example:* 3, 7, 11, 15 (add 4).
- **GP (geometric progression)**: a list where you multiply by the same number each time. *Example:* 2, 6, 18, 54 (multiply by 3).
- **Factorial** $n!$: multiply all whole numbers from 1 to $n$. *Example:* $4! = 4 \times 3 \times 2 \times 1 = 24$.
- **Permutation**: an arrangement where order matters. *Example:* AB and BA are two different arrangements.
- **Combination**: a selection where order does not matter. *Example:* choosing {A, B} is the same as {B, A}.
- **Mean, median, mode**: the average; the middle value after sorting; the most frequent value. *Example:* for 2, 3, 3, 8: mean $= 16 \div 4 = 4$, median $= 3$, mode $= 3$.
- **Probability**: favourable cases divided by all equally likely cases. *Example:* getting a 6 on a dice $= \frac16$.
- **Harmonic mean**: the average used for rates like speed. For two numbers $u$ and $v$ it is $\frac{2uv}{u+v}$. *Example:* for 40 and 60 it is 48, not 50.

### Formulas, explained

**1. Percentage as a multiplier**

$$\text{new} = \text{old} \times \left(1 + \frac{x}{100}\right)$$

- **Read it as:** "a rise of $x$ percent multiplies the old value by one plus $x$ over 100."
- **Symbols:** $x$ is the percent change. Use a minus sign for a fall.
- **Why it makes sense:** A 10% rise keeps the whole 100% and adds 10%, so you get 110% $= 1.1$ times.
- **Use it when:** prices rise or fall, or one side of a shape grows. If area $= L \times W$ and $W$ becomes $1.1W$, the area becomes $1.1$ times.

**2. Two percentage changes in a row**

$$\text{net change} = a + b + \frac{ab}{100} \;\text{percent}$$

- **Read it as:** "net change equals a plus b plus a times b over 100."
- **Symbols:** $a$ and $b$ are the two changes in percent (negative for a fall).
- **Why it makes sense:** The second change acts on the already-changed amount, so there is an extra "change on change" term $\frac{ab}{100}$.
- **Use it when:** a price rises 10% and then falls 10%. Net $= 10 - 10 + \frac{(10)(-10)}{100} = -1\%$, a loss.

**3. Exponent rules**

$$a^m \cdot a^n = a^{m+n}, \qquad \frac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m-n}, \qquad (a^m)^n = a^{mn}$$

- **Read it as:** "multiply means add the powers; divide means subtract them; a power of a power means multiply them."
- **Symbols:** $a$ is the base, $m$ and $n$ are the exponents.
- **Why it makes sense:** $2^2 \cdot 2^3 = (2 \cdot 2)(2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2)$ is five 2s, so it is $2^5$.
- **Use it when:** an equation has powers. **Write everything with the same base**, then set the exponents equal.

**4. Log rules**

$$\log(ab) = \log a + \log b, \qquad \log a^k = k \log a, \qquad a^{\log b} = b^{\log a}$$

- **Read it as:** "the log of a product is the sum of logs; a power comes down in front; and a to the log b equals b to the log a."
- **Symbols:** $a, b$ are positive numbers; $k$ is any number; all logs use the same base (often 10).
- **Why it makes sense:** Logs turn multiplication into addition. For the last rule, take log of both sides: both give $\log a \cdot \log b$.
- **Use it when:** you must compare expressions like $m^{\log n}$ and $n^{\log m}$ (they are equal).

**5. Sums of series**

$$S_n = \frac{n}{2}\big(2a + (n-1)d\big) \quad \text{(AP)}$$

$$S_\infty = \frac{a}{1-r}, \quad -1 < r < 1 \quad \text{(infinite GP)}$$

- **Read it as:** "AP sum is n over 2 times (two a plus (n minus 1) d). An infinite GP sums to a over (1 minus r)."
- **Symbols:** $a$ is the first term, $d$ the common difference, $n$ the number of terms, $r$ the common ratio.
- **Why it makes sense:** For an AP, pair the first and last terms: each pair has the same sum. For a GP with $r = \frac12$: $\frac12 + \frac14 + \cdots$ fills one whole cake, and $\frac{1/2}{1 - 1/2} = 1$.
- **Use it when:** a list of numbers keeps going. If two patterns are mixed, **split them** into two series.

**6. Counting**

$${}^nP_r = \frac{n!}{(n-r)!}, \qquad {}^nC_r = \binom{n}{r} = \frac{n!}{r!\,(n-r)!}$$

- **Read it as:** "n P r is arrangements of r things from n; n C r is selections of r things from n."
- **Symbols:** $n$ is the total number of items; $r$ is how many you pick.
- **Why it makes sense:** For arrangements of 2 from 4 letters, the first place has 4 choices and the second has 3, so $4 \times 3 = 12 = \frac{4!}{2!}$. A selection ignores order, so divide by the $r!$ orders of the same group.
- **Use it when:** you form numbers, pick teams, or arrange people.

**7. Binomial probability**

$$P(\text{exactly } k \text{ of } n) = \binom{n}{k} p^k (1-p)^{n-k}$$

- **Read it as:** "choose which k trials succeed, times p to the k, times (1 minus p) to the rest."
- **Symbols:** $n$ trials, $k$ successes, $p$ the chance of success in one trial.
- **Why it makes sense:** Each pattern (like GGB) has the same chance, and $\binom{n}{k}$ counts the patterns.
- **Use it when:** coins, children (boy/girl), pass/fail questions.

**8. Mean, median, mode**

$$\text{mean} = \frac{\text{sum of values}}{\text{number of values}}$$

- **Read it as:** "add everything, divide by how many."
- **Median:** sort the values and take the middle one (the 3rd of 5). **Mode:** the value seen most often.
- **Use it when:** you must rebuild a data set. Start from the sum: sum $=$ mean $\times$ count.

**9. Triangle area and circle angles**

$$\text{Area} = \frac12 ab \sin C, \qquad \text{central angle} = 2 \times \text{inscribed angle}$$

- **Read it as:** "half times two sides times sine of the angle between them"; "the angle at the centre is twice the angle at the edge on the same arc".
- **Symbols:** $a, b$ are two sides; $C$ is the angle between them.
- **Use it when:** a triangle has two radii of a circle as sides. Then area $= \frac12 r^2 \sin\theta$.

**10. Divisibility rules**
- Divisible by 3 (or 9): the **digit sum** is divisible by 3 (or 9). *Example:* 471: $4+7+1 = 12$, so yes for 3.
- Divisible by 4: the last two digits form a multiple of 4. By 8: the last three digits.
- **Use it when:** you count numbers with a divisibility condition.

**11. Other everyday formulas (common in GA papers of all branches)**

| Topic | Formula | Tiny example |
|---|---|---|
| Speed | $\text{speed} = \frac{\text{distance}}{\text{time}}$ | 120 km in 2 h is 60 km/h |
| Average speed (equal distances) | $\frac{2uv}{u+v}$ | 40 and 60 km/h give 48 km/h |
| Work together | $\frac{1}{T} = \frac{1}{A} + \frac{1}{B}$ | 10 days and 15 days give 6 days |
| Profit percent | $\frac{\text{SP} - \text{CP}}{\text{CP}} \times 100$ | CP 400, SP 500 gives 25% |
| Simple interest | $\frac{P \times R \times T}{100}$ | 10000 at 10% for 2 years is 2000 |
| Compound amount | $P\left(1 + \frac{R}{100}\right)^T$ | 10000 at 10% for 2 years is 12100 |

Here SP is the selling price, CP is the cost price, $P$ the principal (money lent), $R$ the rate per year in percent, and $T$ the time in years.

### Where the formulas come from
Every shortcut above is built from one or two basic ideas: "percent means out of 100", "a power is repeated multiplication", "pair things up", and "count every case once". Here we build each one step by step.

**A. Percentage as a multiplier (formula 1)**

Start with a number. A 500-rupee shirt gets a 20% rise in price.

1. "20 percent" means $\frac{20}{100}$, because "per cent" means "per hundred".
2. The rise is $500 \times \frac{20}{100} = 100$ rupees, because "20% of 500" means 20 parts out of every 100 parts of 500.
3. The new price is $500 + 100 = 600$, because new = old + rise.
4. Now do the same with symbols: rise $= \text{old} \times \frac{x}{100}$.
5. So new $= \text{old} + \text{old} \times \frac{x}{100}$.
6. Take "old" out as a common factor, because $p \cdot 1 + p \cdot q = p(1 + q)$:

$$\text{new} = \text{old} \times \left(1 + \frac{x}{100}\right)$$

**Result:** a rise of $x\%$ is the same as multiplying by $1 + \frac{x}{100}$. For a fall, $x$ is negative, so a 20% fall multiplies by $0.8$.

**B. Two percentage changes in a row: $a + b + \frac{ab}{100}$ (formula 2)**

Start with numbers: a price of 100 rises 10%, then rises 20%.

1. After the first rise, the price is $100 \times 1.1 = 110$, by formula 1.
2. The second rise is 20% of **110**, not of 100. So the price becomes $110 \times 1.2 = 132$.
3. The net change is $132 - 100 = 32\%$. Notice $32 = 10 + 20 + 2$, and the extra 2 is "20% of the first 10".

Now the same with symbols.

1. After two changes, new $= \text{old} \times \left(1 + \frac{a}{100}\right)\left(1 + \frac{b}{100}\right)$, because each change is one multiplication (formula 1).
2. Multiply the two brackets out, term by term:

$$\left(1 + \frac{a}{100}\right)\left(1 + \frac{b}{100}\right) = 1 + \frac{a}{100} + \frac{b}{100} + \frac{ab}{10000}$$

3. Write this as $1 + \frac{\text{net}}{100}$ to read off the net percent. This is allowed because formula 1 says any multiplier $1 + \frac{x}{100}$ means a change of $x\%$.
4. Multiply the extra part by 100: net $= a + b + \frac{ab}{100}$.

**Result:** $a + b$ is the "simple" part. The term $\frac{ab}{100}$ is the change on the change: the second percent acting on the first percent's extra amount. When one change is a rise and the other a fall, $ab$ is negative, so you always lose a little.

**C. Exponent rules (formula 3)**

1. $2^2 \cdot 2^3 = (2 \cdot 2) \cdot (2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2)$. Count the 2s: there are $2 + 3 = 5$. So $2^2 \cdot 2^3 = 2^5$. In general $a^m \cdot a^n = a^{m+n}$, because you just count how many $a$'s are multiplied.
2. $\frac{2^5}{2^3} = \frac{2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2}{2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2}$. Three 2s cancel from top and bottom, leaving $2^2$. So $\frac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m-n}$, because each $a$ below cancels one $a$ above.
3. $(2^3)^2 = 2^3 \cdot 2^3 = 2^{3+3} = 2^6$, by step 1. Two groups of three 2s is $3 \times 2$ twos. So $(a^m)^n = a^{mn}$.
4. Why $a^0 = 1$: by step 2, $\frac{a^3}{a^3} = a^{3-3} = a^0$. But anything divided by itself is 1. So $a^0 = 1$.
5. Why "same base, so equal exponents": the function $2^x$ always grows as $x$ grows, so two different powers can never give the same value. So $2^p = 2^q$ forces $p = q$.

**D. Log rules (formula 4)**

A log is just an exponent, so every log rule is an exponent rule in disguise. Use base 10 here.

1. Let $\log a = x$ and $\log b = y$. By the meaning of log, $a = 10^x$ and $b = 10^y$.
2. Then $ab = 10^x \cdot 10^y = 10^{x+y}$, by the exponent rule C1.
3. So $\log(ab) = x + y = \log a + \log b$, because the log of $10^{x+y}$ is $x + y$.
4. Power rule: $a^k = (10^x)^k = 10^{kx}$, by rule C3. So $\log a^k = kx = k \log a$.
5. Swap rule. Take the log of $a^{\log b}$. By step 4, it is $(\log b)(\log a)$.
6. Take the log of $b^{\log a}$. By step 4, it is $(\log a)(\log b)$.
7. Both logs are the same product, because $pq = qp$. Two positive numbers with equal logs are equal (same reason as C5). So $a^{\log b} = b^{\log a}$.

**Check with numbers:** $100^{\log 10} = 100^1 = 100$, and $10^{\log 100} = 10^2 = 100$. Equal, as promised.

**E. Sum of an AP (formula 5, first part)**

Start with $1 + 2 + 3 + \cdots + 10$.

1. Write the sum forwards and backwards, one under the other: $1 + 2 + \cdots + 10$ and $10 + 9 + \cdots + 1$.
2. Add each column: $1 + 10 = 11$, $2 + 9 = 11$, and so on. Every column gives 11, because as one row goes up by 1 the other goes down by 1.
3. There are 10 columns, so twice the sum is $10 \times 11 = 110$. The sum is $55$.

Now in symbols, with first term $a$, difference $d$ and $n$ terms.

1. The last term is $a + (n-1)d$, because you add $d$ a total of $n - 1$ times to get from the 1st term to the $n$-th.
2. Each column sum is first $+$ last $= a + a + (n-1)d = 2a + (n-1)d$, for the same reason as step 2 above.
3. There are $n$ columns, so $2S_n = n\big(2a + (n-1)d\big)$. Divide by 2:

$$S_n = \frac{n}{2}\big(2a + (n-1)d\big) = \frac{n}{2}(\text{first} + \text{last})$$

**F. Sum of an infinite GP (formula 5, second part)**

1. Let $S = a + ar + ar^2 + ar^3 + \cdots$.
2. Multiply every term by $r$: $rS = ar + ar^2 + ar^3 + \cdots$. This is allowed because multiplying both sides of an equation by the same number keeps it true.
3. Subtract the second line from the first. Every term after $a$ appears in both lines and cancels, so $S - rS = a$.
4. Take $S$ out as a common factor: $S(1 - r) = a$. Divide by $1 - r$:

$$S = \frac{a}{1 - r}$$

5. Why we need $-1 < r < 1$: then the terms keep shrinking, like halving a cake. If $r = 2$, the terms are $1, 2, 4, 8, \ldots$, which grow forever, so there is no finite sum, and the formula would wrongly give $\frac{1}{1-2} = -1$.

**Check:** $a = \frac12$, $r = \frac12$ gives $\frac{1/2}{1/2} = 1$, the whole cake.

**G. Permutations and combinations (formula 6)**

Start with 4 letters A, B, C, D, and fill 2 places in order.

1. The first place can take any of 4 letters.
2. For each of those, the second place has 3 letters left. So there are $4 \times 3 = 12$ ordered pairs. We multiply because every first choice combines with every second choice (the **multiplication principle**).
3. In symbols: $n$ choices, then $n - 1$, down to $n - r + 1$, giving ${}^nP_r = n(n-1)\cdots(n-r+1)$.
4. This product equals $\frac{n!}{(n-r)!}$, because the $(n-r)!$ at the bottom cancels the unwanted tail. *Example:* $\frac{4!}{2!} = \frac{24}{2} = 12$.
5. Now selections. In the 12 ordered pairs, AB and BA are the same team. Every team of 2 appears $2! = 2$ times, so there are $\frac{12}{2} = 6$ teams.
6. In general every group of $r$ can be ordered in $r!$ ways (by step 3 with $r$ places), so divide by $r!$:

$${}^nC_r = \frac{{}^nP_r}{r!} = \frac{n!}{r!\,(n-r)!}$$

**H. Binomial probability (formula 7)**

Start with 3 children, each a girl with chance $\frac12$. What is the chance of exactly 2 girls?

1. List the patterns with 2 girls: GGB, GBG, BGG. There are 3, which is $\binom{3}{2}$, because we only choose **which** 2 of the 3 places are girls.
2. Each pattern has chance $\frac12 \cdot \frac12 \cdot \frac12 = \frac18$, because the children are independent, so chances multiply.
3. The patterns cannot happen together, so their chances add: $3 \times \frac18 = \frac38$.

Now in general, with $n$ trials and success chance $p$.

1. One fixed pattern with $k$ successes and $n - k$ failures has chance $p^k (1-p)^{n-k}$, by multiplying independent chances.
2. Every such pattern has the same chance, because only the order differs.
3. The number of patterns is $\binom{n}{k}$, by formula 6 (choose which $k$ places succeed). Multiply, and you get formula 7.

**I. Mean (formula 8)**

1. The mean is the "fair share": if 4 friends have 2, 3, 3 and 8 rupees and they pool it, each gets $16 \div 4 = 4$.
2. So mean $= \frac{\text{sum}}{\text{count}}$, because sharing equally means dividing the total by the number of people.
3. Multiply both sides by the count: sum $=$ mean $\times$ count. This is the rearranged form that rebuilds data sets.

**J. Triangle area $\frac12 ab \sin C$ (formula 9)**

1. You already know area $= \frac12 \times \text{base} \times \text{height}$, because a triangle is half of a rectangle with the same base and height.
2. Take side $a$ as the base. Drop a straight line from the far corner down to the base: that is the height $h$.
3. This makes a right-angled triangle with longest side $b$ and angle $C$. By the meaning of sine, $\sin C = \frac{h}{b}$, so $h = b \sin C$.
4. Put this into step 1: area $= \frac12 \cdot a \cdot b \sin C$.

**Check:** if $C = 90^\circ$, $\sin C = 1$ and the area is $\frac12 ab$, the usual right-triangle area.

**K. Central angle $= 2 \times$ inscribed angle (formula 9)**

Take a circle with centre O. Draw a diameter from P through O to Q, and a point A on the circle.

1. The inscribed angle at P is $\angle APQ$. Call it $x$.
2. OP and OA are both radii, so triangle OPA has two equal sides. In such a triangle, the two base angles are equal, so $\angle OAP = x$ too.
3. The angles of triangle OPA add to $180^\circ$, so $\angle POA = 180^\circ - 2x$.
4. P, O and Q lie on one straight line, which makes $180^\circ$. So $\angle AOQ = 180^\circ - (180^\circ - 2x) = 2x$.
5. $\angle AOQ$ is the central angle on the same arc AQ. So central $= 2 \times$ inscribed.

For a general inscribed angle, split it with the diameter into two such pieces and add them. The full proof of every case is not needed for GATE.

**L. Divisibility by 3 (formula 10)**

Take 471.

1. Write it by place value: $471 = 4 \times 100 + 7 \times 10 + 1$.
2. Split each power of 10 as "a multiple of 9, plus 1": $100 = 99 + 1$ and $10 = 9 + 1$.
3. So $471 = (4 \times 99 + 7 \times 9) + (4 + 7 + 1)$.
4. The first bracket is a multiple of 9, so also of 3. So 471 is divisible by 3 exactly when $4 + 7 + 1$ is.
5. Every power of 10 is "a multiple of 9, plus 1" (1000 = 999 + 1, and so on), so this works for any number. That is why the same digit-sum rule works for 9.

For 4: $100$ is divisible by 4, so all the hundreds can be ignored and only the last two digits matter. For 8, the same idea works with 1000.

**M. Average speed is the harmonic mean, $\frac{2uv}{u+v}$**

Start with numbers: go 120 km at 40 km/h and return 120 km at 60 km/h.

1. Time going $= \frac{120}{40} = 3$ hours, because time $= \frac{\text{distance}}{\text{speed}}$.
2. Time returning $= \frac{120}{60} = 2$ hours.
3. Average speed $= \frac{\text{total distance}}{\text{total time}} = \frac{240}{5} = 48$ km/h, because average speed is defined by the whole trip.
4. It is less than 50, because you spend **more time** (3 hours) at the slow speed. The slow speed gets more weight.

Now in symbols, with distance $d$ each way at speeds $u$ and $v$.

1. Total time $= \frac{d}{u} + \frac{d}{v} = \frac{d(u + v)}{uv}$, by adding the fractions over the common bottom $uv$.
2. Average $= \frac{2d}{d(u+v)/(uv)} = \frac{2uv}{u+v}$. The $d$ cancels, so the answer does not depend on the distance.

This is called the **harmonic mean** of $u$ and $v$. Use it only when the **distances** are equal. If the **times** are equal, the plain average $\frac{u+v}{2}$ is correct.

**N. Working together, $\frac1T = \frac1A + \frac1B$**

1. If A finishes a job in 10 days, A does $\frac{1}{10}$ of the job each day. This "rate" is the key idea.
2. B, who takes 15 days, does $\frac{1}{15}$ per day.
3. Together they do $\frac{1}{10} + \frac{1}{15} = \frac{3}{30} + \frac{2}{30} = \frac{5}{30} = \frac16$ per day, because work done adds up.
4. One-sixth per day means the job takes 6 days. In symbols, $\frac{1}{T} = \frac1A + \frac1B$.

Notice the link: this is the same "add the rates, not the times" idea as average speed.

**O. Simple and compound interest**

1. Simple interest: each year you earn $\frac{R}{100}$ of the **original** $P$. Over $T$ years, that is $P \times \frac{R}{100} \times T = \frac{PRT}{100}$.
2. Compound interest: each year's interest is added to the money, and the next year's percent acts on the new total. So each year is one multiplication by $1 + \frac{R}{100}$ (formula 1).
3. After $T$ years you multiply $T$ times: amount $= P\left(1 + \frac{R}{100}\right)^T$. With 10000 at 10% for 2 years: $10000 \times 1.1 \times 1.1 = 12100$.

Compound interest is just formula 2 applied again and again. The extra 100 rupees over simple interest (12100 vs 12000) is exactly the "change on change" term: $\frac{10 \times 10}{100} = 1\%$ of 10000.

**P. Profit percent**

Profit percent is just percentage change (formula 1 read backwards) with CP as "old" and SP as "new". We divide by CP because the profit is compared with what you paid.

### Examples
**Example 1 (easy):** A phone costs 20,000 rupees. Its price rises 10%, and then falls 10%. What is the final price?

1. After the rise: $20000 \times 1.1 = 22000$.
2. After the fall: $22000 \times 0.9 = 19800$.
3. Check with formula 2: net $= 10 - 10 + \frac{-100}{100} = -1\%$. And $1\%$ of 20000 is 200, so $20000 - 200 = 19800$.

**Result:** 19,800 rupees (not 20,000).

**Example 2 (medium):** If $4^x \cdot 8 = 2^{x^2}$, find $\frac{5^{x^2}}{25^x}$.

1. Use the same base 2. $4^x = (2^2)^x = 2^{2x}$ and $8 = 2^3$. So the left side is $2^{2x+3}$.
2. $2^{2x+3} = 2^{x^2}$, so $x^2 = 2x + 3$, which means $x^2 - 2x = 3$.
3. $25^x = (5^2)^x = 5^{2x}$. So $\frac{5^{x^2}}{5^{2x}} = 5^{x^2 - 2x} = 5^3 = 125$.

**Result:** 125. We never needed $x$. (Check: $x = 3$ gives $\frac{5^9}{5^6} = 5^3$.)

**Example 3 (GATE level):** Find $S = 1 + \frac14 + \frac15 + \frac{1}{16} + \frac{1}{25} + \frac{1}{64} + \frac{1}{125} + \cdots$

1. After the 1, the terms alternate: powers of $\frac14$ and powers of $\frac15$.
2. Powers of $\frac14$: $a = \frac14$, $r = \frac14$. Sum $= \frac{1/4}{1 - 1/4} = \frac{1/4}{3/4} = \frac13$.
3. Powers of $\frac15$: $a = \frac15$, $r = \frac15$. Sum $= \frac{1/5}{1 - 1/5} = \frac{1/5}{4/5} = \frac14$.
4. $S = 1 + \frac13 + \frac14 = \frac{12}{12} + \frac{4}{12} + \frac{3}{12} = \frac{19}{12}$.

**Result:** $\frac{19}{12}$.

**Example 4 (GATE level):** Five integers from 0 to 10 (repeats allowed) have mean 6, median 7 and a single mode of 9. Ignoring order, how many such sets exist?

1. Sort them: $a \le b \le c \le d \le e$. The median is the middle one, so $c = 7$.
2. The mode 9 must appear at least twice. Only $d$ and $e$ can be bigger than 7, so $d = e = 9$.
3. Sum $= 5 \times 6 = 30$. So $a + b = 30 - 7 - 9 - 9 = 5$.
4. Pairs with $a \le b$: $(0,5)$, $(1,4)$, $(2,3)$. None repeats a value, so 9 stays the only mode.

**Result:** 3 sets.

**Example 5 (medium):** A cyclist rides to a village at 12 km/h and returns on the same road at 18 km/h. What is the average speed?

1. The distances are equal, so use the harmonic mean (section M).
2. Average $= \frac{2 \times 12 \times 18}{12 + 18} = \frac{432}{30} = 14.4$ km/h.
3. Sanity check: it is below the plain average 15, because more time is spent at 12 km/h.

**Result:** 14.4 km/h.

### Common mistakes
> **Common mistake:** Adding percentages directly. A 10% rise and a 10% fall is a 1% loss, not zero. Always multiply: $1.1 \times 0.9 = 0.99$.

> **Common mistake:** Solving for $x$ when only an expression is asked. It wastes time and creates errors. Check if the expression can be built from the given equation.

> **Common mistake:** Using $\frac{a}{1-r}$ when $r$ is 1 or more. The infinite GP sum works only when $-1 < r < 1$.

> **Common mistake:** Forgetting that a "single mode" must be unique. If another value appears as often as the mode, that case is not allowed (2026 Q.7).

> **Common mistake:** Allowing 0 as the first digit when counting numbers. Check whether 0 is among the digits.

> **Common mistake:** Averaging two speeds as $\frac{u+v}{2}$ when the distances are equal. Use $\frac{2uv}{u+v}$.

### Summary
- First, "percent" means "out of 100", so a change of $x\%$ is a multiplication by $1 + \frac{x}{100}$.
- Two changes are two multiplications, and multiplying the brackets out gives $a + b + \frac{ab}{100}$; compound interest is the same idea repeated.
- A power is repeated multiplication, so exponent rules come from counting factors, and a log is an exponent, so log rules are exponent rules in disguise.
- Series sums come from tricks: pairing first and last terms (AP), and multiplying by $r$ and subtracting (GP).
- Counting starts from "multiply the choices"; dividing by $r!$ removes repeated orders, and the binomial formula counts patterns times the chance of one pattern.
- Rates (speed, work) add as rates, not as times, which is why average speed over equal distances is the harmonic mean.
- Next topic builds on this: Data Interpretation reuses percentages and the weighted mean, and in ML these same counting and probability ideas become the binomial distribution.

### Quick self-check
1. Find $1 + \frac13 + \frac19 + \frac{1}{27} + \cdots$
**Answer:** $\frac{1}{1 - 1/3} = \frac{1}{2/3} = \frac32$.

2. How many ways can 3 people sit in 3 chairs in a row?
**Answer:** $3! = 3 \times 2 \times 1 = 6$.

3. Two coins are tossed. What is the probability of exactly one head?
**Answer:** HT and TH out of 4 outcomes, so $\frac24 = \frac12$.

4. A price rises 20% and then rises 20% again. What is the total rise?
**Answer:** $20 + 20 + \frac{20 \times 20}{100} = 44\%$.

## Analytical / Logical Reasoning

### The big idea
Logical reasoning means finding what **must** be true from a few given facts. You do not guess. You test each possibility, and you throw away the ones that break a rule.

### Before you start
You need only a few simple ideas. Each one is explained from zero.

**1. A statement.** A statement is a sentence that is either true or false. "Delhi is in India" is a true statement. "Will you come?" is not a statement, because it is a question.

**2. "If ... then".** "If P then Q" promises: whenever P happens, Q happens too. It promises nothing about what happens when P does not happen. *Example:* "If it rains, the road is wet" says nothing about a dry day.

**3. A function.** A function is a rule that turns an input into exactly one output. *Example:* $f(x) = 2x$ turns 3 into 6. We write $f(3) = 6$, read "f of 3 is 6". Speed at time $t$ or weight at age $a$ are functions too.

**4. The symbols $<$, $>$, $\le$, $\ge$.** $a < b$ means "a is less than b". $a \le b$ means "a is less than or equal to b". *Example:* $3 < 5$ and $5 \le 5$ are both true.

**5. Sets and circles (Venn diagrams).** A set is a group of things, like "all doctors". We draw each set as a circle. Things inside the circle belong to the set, and an overlap of two circles holds things that belong to both.

**6. "Must" versus "can".** "Must be true" means true in **every** possible case. "Can be true" means true in **at least one** case. GATE almost always asks about "must".

### Intuition
Think of a cricket knockout tournament. A team that loses goes home. With 8 teams, how many matches are needed to find a winner?

Each match sends exactly one team home. To leave one winner, 7 teams must go home. So there are **7 matches**, and you did not need to draw the full fixture.

GATE logic puzzles work in the same way. Usually there are only 2, 3 or 4 possible cases. You try one case and follow it step by step; if it breaks a rule, cross it out, and the case that survives is the answer.

> **Intuition:** Logic is like a lock with a few keys. Try each key. Most keys will not turn. The one that turns is your answer, and you can be sure because you tried all the others.

Here is a second picture. A child grows taller every year until about age 18. Two children of **different ages** below 18 cannot have exactly the same height, because the older one has grown more.

This is the idea behind a **monotonic** function. It also solves GATE 2025 Q.7.

### Words you need
- **Deduction**: reasoning where the conclusion **must** be true if the facts are true. *Example:* All cats have tails. Tom is a cat. So Tom has a tail.
- **Induction**: guessing a general rule from examples. It is likely, but not certain. *Example:* "The sun rose every day I remember, so it will rise tomorrow."
- **Case analysis**: listing all possibilities and checking each one. *Example:* Game 1 was either between two Class 5 students or two Class 4 students.
- **Contradiction**: a result that breaks a given fact. It shows a case is impossible.
- **Monotonically increasing**: always going up as the input goes up. *Example:* the total runs in an innings never go down.
- **Monotonically decreasing**: always going down. *Example:* the fuel in a car's tank during a drive with no refuelling.
- **Peak**: the input where a function stops rising and starts falling. The output is highest there.
- **Syllogism**: two statements, and you check which conclusion follows. *Example:* "All A are B. All B are C. So all A are C."
- **Converse**: from "If P then Q", the statement "If Q then P". It does **not** mean the same thing.
- **Contrapositive**: from "If P then Q", the statement "If not Q then not P". It means the same thing.
- **Counterexample**: one example that shows a "must be true" claim is false.

### Formulas, explained
Logic has rules instead of formulas.

**1. The knockout rule**

$$\text{games needed} = n - 1$$

- **Read it as:** "with n players, n minus 1 games leave one winner."
- **Symbols:** $n$ is the number of players.
- **Why it makes sense:** Each game removes exactly one player. You must remove $n - 1$ players.
- **Use it when:** losers leave the game. With 4 players and 3 games, exactly one player never loses.

**2. Monotonic functions never repeat a value**

$$a < b \;\Rightarrow\; f(a) < f(b) \quad (\text{strictly increasing})$$

- **Read it as:** "if a is less than b, then f of a is less than f of b."
- **Symbols:** $f$ is a function (a rule that turns an input into an output), and $a, b$ are inputs.
- **Why it makes sense:** If the output always goes up, two different inputs give two different outputs.
- **Use it when:** a function rises and then falls (one peak). Then two different inputs with the **same** output must lie on **opposite sides** of the peak.

**3. If-then logic**

$$(P \Rightarrow Q) \;\equiv\; (\text{not } Q \Rightarrow \text{not } P)$$

- **Read it as:** "If P then Q is the same as: if not Q, then not P."
- **Symbols:** $P$ and $Q$ are statements; $\Rightarrow$ means "implies"; $\equiv$ means "means the same as".
- **Why it makes sense:** "If it rains, the road is wet." So if the road is dry, it did not rain. But a wet road does **not** prove rain (a water tanker could have passed).
- **Use it when:** an option turns "if P then Q" around into "if Q then P". That option is a trap.

**4. Syllogisms with circles**
- "All A are B": draw the A circle inside the B circle.
- "Some A are B": the two circles overlap.
- "No A is B": the circles are apart.
- A conclusion follows only if it is true in **every** possible drawing.

### Where the formulas come from
Logic rules are not memorised facts. Each one comes from counting carefully or from checking every case. Here is how each rule is built.

**A. Why a knockout needs $n - 1$ games (formula 1)**

Start small and count.

1. With 2 players: 1 game, and 1 player goes home. $2 - 1 = 1$.
2. With 4 players: semi-finals (2 games) and a final (1 game), so 3 games. $4 - 1 = 3$.
3. Notice that every game, without exception, sends exactly **one** player home. This is true because a game has one loser and losers leave.
4. At the end, exactly one player (the winner) is left. So $n - 1$ players went home.
5. One game per player sent home means $n - 1$ games, because games and losers are matched one to one.

**Result:** games $= n - 1$. This works even when $n$ is not a power of 2 (byes do not change it), because the argument only counts losers.

**B. Why a strictly increasing function never repeats a value (formula 2)**

1. "Strictly increasing" means: whenever $a < b$, we have $f(a) < f(b)$. This is the definition.
2. Take two different inputs $a \ne b$. One of them is smaller; call it $a$, so $a < b$.
3. By step 1, $f(a) < f(b)$. So $f(a) \ne f(b)$.
4. So two different inputs always give different outputs. The same works for "strictly decreasing", with $>$ instead of $<$.

**C. Why equal values sit on opposite sides of a peak**

Use the car example: speed rises until minute 10, then falls. Suppose $t_1 < t_2$ and the speeds are equal.

1. Both cannot be before 10, because the speed is strictly rising there, so by B the two speeds would differ.
2. Both cannot be after 10, because the speed is strictly falling there, so again by B the two speeds would differ.
3. Neither can be exactly 10, because at the peak the speed is higher than at every other moment, so no other time can match it.
4. Only one case is left: $t_1 < 10 < t_2$. Because we checked every other case and each broke a rule, this one must be true.

This is case analysis at work: list all cases, cross out the impossible ones, and what remains is forced.

**D. Why "If P then Q" equals "If not Q then not P" (formula 3)**

The full truth-table proof is not needed for GATE, but a short case check shows it.

1. "If P then Q" forbids only one situation: P true and Q false. All other situations are allowed.
2. Now suppose Q is false (the road is dry).
3. If P were true (it rained), we would be in the one forbidden situation. So P must be false.
4. That is exactly "If not Q then not P". Both statements forbid the same single situation, so they mean the same thing.
5. The converse "If Q then P" forbids a **different** situation (Q true, P false: wet road, no rain). The first statement allows that, which is why the converse is a trap.

**E. Why a syllogism needs "every drawing" (formula 4)**

1. Each "All / Some / No" statement allows several different pictures. "Some graduates are singers" only says the circles overlap; it does not say **where**.
2. A conclusion **must** follow only if it is true no matter how you draw the allowed pictures. This is the meaning of "must" from "Before you start".
3. So the method is: try to draw a picture where the facts hold but the conclusion fails. If you can, that picture is a counterexample and the conclusion does not follow.
4. "All A are B, all B are C" puts A inside B inside C. Any drawing that keeps these rules has A inside C, so "All A are C" always follows.

### Examples
**Example 1 (easy):** 16 teams play a knockout tournament. How many matches are played?

1. Each match sends exactly one team home.
2. To leave 1 winner, $16 - 1 = 15$ teams go home.

**Result:** 15 matches.

**Example 2 (medium):** "All doctors are graduates. Some graduates are singers." Does "Some doctors are singers" follow?

1. Draw Doctors inside Graduates.
2. The Singers circle overlaps Graduates somewhere.
3. Draw that overlap outside the Doctors circle. Both facts still hold, but no doctor is a singer. This is a counterexample.

**Result:** No. But "Some singers are graduates" does follow. It is the same as the second statement, turned around.

**Example 3 (GATE level):** A car's speed rises strictly for the first 10 minutes of a trip, and then falls strictly. At times $t_1 < t_2$ the speed is the same. Which is true? (A) $t_1 < t_2 < 10$ (B) $t_1 < 10 < t_2$ (C) $10 < t_1 < t_2$ (D) $t_1 = 10$

1. Case (A): both before 10. Speed is rising, so speed at $t_1$ < speed at $t_2$. Not equal. Rejected.
2. Case (C): both after 10. Speed is falling, so speed at $t_1$ > speed at $t_2$. Rejected.
3. Case (D): at $t_1 = 10$ the speed is at its highest. Any other time has a lower speed. Rejected.

**Result:** (B). One time is before the peak, and one is after.

**Example 4 (medium):** "If a student is in the hostel, she has an ID card." Riya has no ID card. What can you say?

1. Let P = "Riya is in the hostel" and Q = "Riya has an ID card".
2. We know "not Q". By the contrapositive, "not Q" gives "not P".

**Result:** Riya is not in the hostel.

### Common mistakes
> **Common mistake:** Choosing an option that is **possible** when the question asks what is **necessarily** true. Ask: "Is it true in every case?"

> **Common mistake:** Turning "If P then Q" into "If Q then P". These are different statements.

> **Common mistake:** Stopping after finding one case that works. In a "who must it be" puzzle, check that the other cases really fail.

> **Common mistake:** Forgetting a rule in long puzzles. Write every rule as a short numbered note, and tick it when you check a case.

### How GATE asks it
- **2025 Q.7 (2 marks):** weight rises until age 50 and falls after it. Two brothers of different ages weigh the same, so $a_1 < 50 < a_2$. This tested the one-peak monotonic idea.
- **2026 Q.8 (2 marks):** four students play three knockout chess games with rules about class and gender. Case analysis on game 1 (Class 5 pair or Class 4 pair) shows Tanvi never lost.
- Both were 2-mark questions. Expect about one per paper.

> **GATE tip:** In a "who is it" puzzle, make a small table: rows for people, columns for facts (class, boy/girl, won/lost). Fill it as you test each case.

### Summary
- First, we separate "must be true" (every case) from "can be true" (some case); GATE asks for "must".
- The basic tool is case analysis: list every case, follow each one, and cross out the cases that break a rule.
- Counting one clear thing (one loser per game) gives the knockout rule $n - 1$ without drawing anything.
- A strictly rising or falling function never repeats a value, so equal values of a one-peak function must sit on opposite sides of the peak.
- "If P then Q" forbids only "P true, Q false", which is why it equals its contrapositive but not its converse.
- That is why one counterexample is enough to reject a "must" claim.
- Next topic builds on this: the same "check one feature, eliminate the rest" habit drives Spatial Reasoning, and if-then logic returns in AI as propositional logic.

### Quick self-check
1. With 10 players in a knockout, how many games are needed?
**Answer:** $10 - 1 = 9$ games.

2. "If a number ends in 0, it is even." Is "If a number is even, it ends in 0" true?
**Answer:** No. 12 is even but does not end in 0. This is the reverse statement (the converse), not the contrapositive.

3. A function rises until $x = 5$ and then falls. If $f(2) = f(k)$ and $k \ne 2$, is $k$ more or less than 5?
**Answer:** More than 5. Equal values must sit on opposite sides of the peak.

## Spatial / Visual Reasoning

### The big idea
Spatial reasoning means picturing shapes in your head: turning them, flipping them, folding them, cutting them, or joining pieces. Most questions come with a figure. A few turn a picture into a small counting or area problem.

### Before you start
These are the shape ideas this topic uses. Each is explained from zero.

**1. A cube.** A cube is a box with 6 square faces, 12 edges and 8 corners. Each face touches 4 other faces along edges, and is opposite exactly 1 face. *Example:* a Ludo dice.

**2. Angles in a full turn.** Going once around a point is $360^\circ$. A half turn is $180^\circ$ and a quarter turn is $90^\circ$. So if a circle is cut into $n$ equal slices from the centre, each slice has angle $\frac{360^\circ}{n}$.

**3. Area of a triangle with sine.** A triangle with two sides $a$ and $b$ and angle $C$ between them has area $\frac12 ab \sin C$ (see topic: Quantitative Aptitude, where this is built from "half base times height"). Values to know: $\sin 30^\circ = \frac12$, $\sin 60^\circ = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}$.

**4. Radius.** The radius $r$ is the distance from the centre of a circle to its edge. Every point on the circle is the same distance $r$ from the centre.

**5. Powers of 2.** $2^k$ means 2 multiplied by itself $k$ times: $2^1 = 2$, $2^2 = 4$, $2^3 = 8$. Something that doubles $k$ times grows by $2^k$.

**6. Clockwise order.** Clockwise is the direction a clock's hands move. If you walk around a shape and meet features in the order A, B, C clockwise, that order is a fingerprint of the shape's "handedness".

### Intuition
Think of a Ludo dice. The numbers on opposite faces never appear together, because you cannot see the top and the bottom of a dice at the same time. If you see 1, 2 and 3 together, those three faces all touch each other, and this one fact solves most dice questions.

Think of a jigsaw puzzle. When you put the pieces together, the total area does not change. If 12 small triangles make a shape, the shape's area is 12 times one triangle; this solves GATE 2025 Q.8.

Think of a political map of India. Neighbouring states get different colours, so you can see the borders. How few colours can you use? This is the **map colouring** question (GATE 2024 Q.2).

> **Intuition:** Do not try to picture the whole object at once. Pick **one feature** (one pair of opposite faces, one curved edge, one corner) and use it to throw out wrong options.

### Words you need
- **Adjacent faces**: faces of a cube that share an edge. *Example:* the top and front of a box.
- **Opposite faces**: faces that never touch. *Example:* the top and bottom of a box.
- **Net**: a flat paper shape that folds into a solid. *Example:* a cross made of 6 squares folds into a cube.
- **Rotation**: turning a shape around a point. It does not flip it. *Example:* turning a clock face.
- **Reflection (mirror image)**: flipping a shape. Left becomes right. *Example:* your face in a mirror.
- **Cross-section**: the flat shape you see when you cut a solid with a straight cut. *Example:* cutting a cucumber across gives a circle.
- **Regular polygon**: a shape with all sides and all angles equal. *Example:* a square, or a 12-sided dodecagon.
- **Inscribed**: drawn inside a circle so that all corners touch the circle.
- **Isosceles triangle**: a triangle with two equal sides. *Example:* a triangle with two sides equal to the radius $r$.

### Formulas, explained

**1. Opposite faces of a cube from views**

$$\text{seen together} \Rightarrow \text{adjacent (not opposite)}$$

- **Read it as:** "faces seen in the same view touch each other."
- **Why it makes sense:** A view shows one corner, where three faces meet.
- **Use it when:** you get several views of a dice. If a face appears with four different faces, it is opposite the sixth face.
- **In a net:** in a straight strip of 4 squares, the 1st and 3rd are opposite, and the 2nd and 4th are opposite. Two flaps on opposite sides of the strip are opposite.

**2. Area of a regular polygon in a circle**

$$\text{Area} = \frac{n}{2} r^2 \sin\left(\frac{360^\circ}{n}\right)$$

- **Read it as:** "n over 2, times r squared, times sine of 360 degrees over n."
- **Symbols:** $n$ is the number of sides; $r$ is the radius of the circle.
- **Why it makes sense:** Cut the polygon into $n$ triangles from the centre. Each triangle has two sides $r$ and angle $\frac{360^\circ}{n}$ between them, so each has area $\frac12 r^2 \sin\frac{360^\circ}{n}$.
- **Use it when:** a polygon is cut into triangles and rearranged. For $n = 12$: each triangle is $\frac12 r^2 \sin 30^\circ = \frac{r^2}{4}$, and the total is $3r^2$.

**3. Map colouring**
- Treat each region as a dot. Join two dots if the regions share a border line (touching at a corner does not count).
- Three regions that all touch each other need **3 colours**.
- A centre region with an **odd** ring around it (5 regions in a ring) needs **4 colours**. An even ring needs only 3.
- Any flat map needs **at most 4 colours** (the four colour theorem).

**4. Mirror and rotation rules**
- A mirror (vertical) swaps left and right. Top and bottom stay.
- A water image (horizontal mirror) swaps top and bottom.
- A rotation keeps the clockwise order of features. A reflection reverses it.

**5. Paper folding and punching**

$$\text{holes after unfolding} = \text{holes punched} \times 2^{\text{folds}}$$

- **Read it as:** "each fold doubles the holes."
- **Why it makes sense:** A hole punched through two layers makes two holes. Each fold doubles the layers.
- **Use it when:** a sheet is folded, punched and opened. Unfold one step at a time. Each hole is mirrored across the fold line. (This is true when the punch goes through all layers and does not sit on a fold line.)

**6. Common cross-sections**
- Cone cut through its tip and axis: an **isosceles triangle** (two equal sides).
- Cone or cylinder cut parallel to the base: a **circle**.
- Sphere cut anywhere: a **circle**.
- Two cones joined at their bases, cut through both tips: a **rhombus** (4 equal sides).

### Where the formulas come from
Spatial rules come from simple facts about a cube, a circle or a sheet of paper. Here is the reasoning behind each one.

**A. Why faces seen together cannot be opposite (formula 1)**

1. Hold a dice so you see as many faces as possible. You can see at most 3 faces: for example top, front and right.
2. These 3 faces meet at one corner of the cube, because every corner of a cube is where exactly 3 faces meet.
3. Faces that meet at a corner share edges, so they are adjacent.
4. Opposite faces are parallel and never meet, so they can never both be in view. So "seen together" means "not opposite".
5. Each face has exactly 4 neighbours and 1 opposite face (6 faces minus itself minus 4 neighbours $= 1$). So if a face is seen with 4 different faces, the one face never seen with it must be its opposite.

**B. Why the 1st and 3rd squares of a strip are opposite**

1. Fold a strip of 4 squares into a loop, like a paper ring. It wraps around the cube's four sides.
2. Square 2 sits between squares 1 and 3, so squares 1 and 3 are on the two sides of square 2.
3. Going around a loop of 4 faces, the face two steps away is on the far side, because four quarter turns make a full circle and two quarter turns make a half turn. So 1 and 3 are opposite, and in the same way 2 and 4 are opposite.
4. The two flaps fold up and down to close the top and bottom, so they are opposite each other.

**C. Area of a regular polygon (formula 2)**

Start with a regular hexagon in a circle of radius $r$.

1. Join the centre to all 6 corners. This cuts the hexagon into 6 triangles, because each side of the hexagon becomes the outer side of one triangle.
2. Every corner is on the circle, so the two sides of each triangle from the centre are both $r$, by the meaning of radius.
3. The 6 angles at the centre fill a full turn, and they are all equal (the polygon is regular), so each is $\frac{360^\circ}{6} = 60^\circ$.
4. One triangle has area $\frac12 \cdot r \cdot r \cdot \sin 60^\circ$, by the triangle-area formula $\frac12 ab \sin C$.
5. The total is $6 \times \frac12 r^2 \sin 60^\circ$, because area adds when pieces do not overlap.

For $n$ sides, replace 6 by $n$:

$$\text{Area} = n \times \frac12 r^2 \sin\left(\frac{360^\circ}{n}\right) = \frac{n}{2} r^2 \sin\left(\frac{360^\circ}{n}\right)$$

**Check:** a square ($n = 4$) in a circle of radius 1 gives $2 \times 1 \times \sin 90^\circ = 2$. The square's diagonal is 2, so its side is $\sqrt2$ and its area is $(\sqrt2)^2 = 2$. It matches.

**D. Map colouring rules (formula 3)**

1. Three regions that all touch each other: each one borders the other two, so no two can share a colour. That forces 3 colours.
2. A ring of regions around a centre: the centre touches every ring region, so the ring cannot use the centre's colour. That leaves only the other colours for the ring.
3. If only 3 colours exist, the ring has 2 colours to share, so it must alternate: red, blue, red, blue, ...
4. Alternating works only if the ring has an even number of regions. With 5 regions, going around gives red, blue, red, blue, red, and the last red touches the first red. So an odd ring forces a 4th colour.
5. The four colour theorem says 4 colours are always enough for a flat map. The full proof is not needed for GATE (it was first proved with a computer checking many cases). Just use it as the upper limit.

**E. Why each fold doubles the holes (formula 5)**

1. A flat sheet is 1 layer. Fold it once: 2 layers. Fold again: 4 layers. Each fold puts the sheet on top of itself, so the layers double.
2. After $k$ folds there are $2 \times 2 \times \cdots = 2^k$ layers, by repeated doubling.
3. One punch goes through every layer, so it makes one hole in each: $2^k$ holes.
4. When you unfold, each fold line acts like a mirror: a hole on one side has a twin at the same distance on the other side.

**F. Why a mirror reverses the clockwise order (formula 4)**

1. Draw a clock face with 12 at the top and 3 on the right. Going clockwise, you meet 12, 3, 6, 9.
2. Rotate the clock a quarter turn. Every number moves together, so the order is still 12, 3, 6, 9 clockwise. Rotation moves the whole shape without flipping it.
3. Now look in a vertical mirror. The 3 jumps to the left side, while 12 stays at the top. Going clockwise now, you meet 12, 9, 6, 3.
4. The order is reversed. No rotation can do this, so "order reversed" is a sure sign of a mirror image.

**G. Cross-sections (formula 6)**

1. A sphere: let O be the centre and F the point of the cut plane nearest to O. Any cut point X is at distance $r$ from O, and OFX is a right angle, so by Pythagoras $FX = \sqrt{r^2 - OF^2}$. This is the same number for every X, so all cut points are the same distance from F: the cut is a circle.
2. A cone cut through its tip and axis: the cut shows the tip and two slanted edges of equal length (every slanted line from tip to base edge has the same length), so it is an isosceles triangle.
3. Two cones glued at their bases, cut through both tips: you get two such triangles glued along the base. All four slanted sides are equal, so it is a rhombus.

### Examples
**Example 1 (easy):** A square sheet is folded in half, then in half again. One hole is punched through all layers. How many holes appear when it is opened?

1. Two folds give $2^2 = 4$ layers.
2. One punch makes one hole in each layer: $1 \times 4$.

**Result:** 4 holes.

**Example 2 (medium):** A dice shows (view 1) A, B, C; (view 2) A, D, E; (view 3) B, E, F. Find all opposite pairs.

1. A is seen with B, C, D, E. So A touches four faces. The sixth face, **F**, is opposite A.
2. B is seen with A, C, E, F. The face left out is **D**. So B is opposite D.
3. The last pair is **C and E**.

**Result:** A-F, B-D, C-E.

**Example 3 (GATE level):** A regular hexagon is drawn inside a circle of radius $r$. It is cut into 6 triangles from the centre. How many triangles are needed to cover a rectangle of area $\sqrt3\, r^2$?

1. Each triangle has two sides $r$ and angle $\frac{360^\circ}{6} = 60^\circ$.
2. Area of one triangle $= \frac12 r^2 \sin 60^\circ = \frac12 r^2 \cdot \frac{\sqrt3}{2} = \frac{\sqrt3}{4} r^2$.
3. Number needed $= \sqrt3\, r^2 \div \frac{\sqrt3}{4} r^2 = 4$.

**Result:** 4 triangles. (Check: the whole hexagon is $6 \times \frac{\sqrt3}{4} r^2 = \frac{3\sqrt3}{2} r^2$, which is 1.5 of these rectangles.)

### Common mistakes
> **Common mistake:** Calling two faces opposite when they were seen in the same view. Faces seen together always touch.

> **Common mistake:** Counting regions that touch only at a corner as neighbours in map colouring. They can share a colour.

> **Common mistake:** Confusing a rotation with a mirror image. If the order of features goes from clockwise to anticlockwise, it is a mirror image, not a rotation.

> **Common mistake:** Picturing the whole figure and getting lost. Check one clear feature per option and eliminate.

### How GATE asks it
- **2024 Q.2:** minimum colours for a 15-region figure: 3 sectors in a circle, a ring of 4 pieces around them. Answer 4 colours.
- **2024 Q.9 (2 marks):** three views of a dice; find the opposite pairs, then pick the correct net.
- **2024 Q.10 (2 marks):** two cones joined at the base, cut through both tips: a rhombus.
- **2025 Q.4:** overlapping rectangle (1), triangle (2), ellipse (3); each region's number is the sum of the shapes covering it.
- **2025 Q.8 (2 marks):** a regular dodecagon cut into 12 triangles of area $\frac{r^2}{4}$ each, so 3 squares of side $r$ with 4 triangles each.
- **2026 Q.3:** four black pieces join into a square frame; pick the white bird shape whose outline matches every cut edge.

> **GATE tip:** If the figure shows a labelled example region (like "4" inside two shapes), use it first to discover the rule. Then apply it to the unknown regions.

### Summary
- First, a cube has 6 faces, and every corner is where 3 faces meet, so faces seen together touch and each face has exactly one opposite.
- A circle cut into $n$ equal slices gives $n$ triangles with two sides $r$, so a regular polygon's area is $n$ times $\frac12 r^2 \sin\frac{360^\circ}{n}$.
- Map colouring is about neighbours: a triangle of neighbours forces 3 colours, an odd ring around a centre forces 4, and 4 is always enough.
- Each fold doubles the layers, so one punch makes $2^{\text{folds}}$ holes, and each fold line acts like a mirror.
- A rotation keeps clockwise order, while a mirror reverses it, which is the quickest test between them.
- That is why the best method is to pick one clear feature and use it to eliminate options.
- Next topic builds on this: Data Interpretation also asks you to read a figure carefully, and in ML these ideas return as rotations and reflections of vectors in linear algebra.

### Quick self-check
1. How many colours does a map need if 3 regions all touch each other and nothing else?
**Answer:** 3.

2. What shape do you get when you cut a sphere with a plane?
**Answer:** A circle.

3. A regular 12-gon sits in a circle of radius 2 cm. What is its area?
**Answer:** $3r^2 = 3 \times 2^2 = 12$ square cm.

## Data Interpretation

### The big idea
Data Interpretation (DI) gives you data as a table, bar chart, pie chart, line graph or grid. You read the right numbers and do one small calculation: a percentage, a count, an average or a ratio. Most mistakes come from **reading** the data wrongly, not from the maths.

### Before you start
DI reuses ideas from Quantitative Aptitude. Here they are again, from zero.

**1. Percentage.** "Percent" means "out of 100". $x\%$ of a number $N$ is $\frac{x}{100} \times N$. *Example:* 30% of 500 $= 0.3 \times 500 = 150$ (see topic: Quantitative Aptitude).

**2. Mean (average).** Add all values and divide by how many there are. *Example:* the mean of 2, 4, 9 is $\frac{15}{3} = 5$.

**3. The sum symbol $\sum$.** $\sum$ (Greek capital sigma) means "add all of these". *Example:* if $x_1 = 2$, $x_2 = 4$, $x_3 = 9$, then $\sum x_i = 2 + 4 + 9 = 15$. The small $i$ is just a counter: 1, 2, 3, ...

**4. Subscripts.** $x_i$ is read "x sub i". It means "the $i$-th value". *Example:* $x_2$ is the second value.

**5. Angles in a circle.** A full circle is $360^\circ$. Half a circle is $180^\circ$, a quarter is $90^\circ$.

**6. Inequality words.** "At most 4" means $\le 4$ (4 is allowed). "Less than 4" means $< 4$ (4 is not allowed). "At least 4" means $\ge 4$, and "more than 4" means $> 4$.

### Intuition
Think of your school report card. It shows marks in five subjects. Your parents ask, "What is your average?" You add the marks and divide by 5.

They ask, "How many subjects are 80 or above?" You count, and you include any subject with exactly 80. That is DI.

Now think of an election result on TV. A pie chart says Party A got 40% of the **valid** votes. Some votes were invalid, and if you take 40% of **all** votes, your answer is wrong. GATE loves this trap.

> **Intuition:** Before any calculation, ask two questions. "What is the base (the whole)?" and "Does the condition include the boundary value?" These two questions catch most traps.

A third picture: a doctor earns 1000 rupees per patient, minus a fixed amount. To find her average earning, you do not need each day's earning. Find the **average number of patients** first, then put it into the earning formula, because averages pass straight through a straight-line formula.

### Words you need
- **Base (the whole)**: the total that a percentage is taken from. *Example:* "30% of valid votes": the base is valid votes.
- **Pie chart**: a circle cut into slices. Each slice shows a share of the total. The full circle is 100% or 360 degrees.
- **Bar chart**: bars whose heights show amounts. *Example:* the number of shifts with 5, 6, 7 or 8 patients.
- **Frequency**: how many times a value occurs. *Example:* "6 patients" happened in 40 shifts, so its frequency is 40.
- **Weighted mean**: an average where each value counts as many times as it occurs.
- **Percentage points**: the plain difference between two percentages. *Example:* from 20% to 25% is a rise of 5 percentage points (but a 25% relative rise).
- **"At most 4"**: means 4 or less, written $\le 4$. The value 4 itself counts.
- **Straight-line formula**: a rule like $Y = a(X - c)$, where $Y$ changes by a fixed amount for each unit change in $X$. Its graph is a straight line.

### Formulas, explained

**1. Value from a pie chart**

$$\text{value} = \frac{\text{share}}{100} \times \text{correct base}$$

$$\text{share} = \frac{\theta}{360^\circ} \times 100\%$$

- **Read it as:** "value equals share percent of the correct total; a slice of angle theta is theta over 360 of the whole."
- **Symbols:** $\theta$ (theta) is the angle of the slice in degrees.
- **Why it makes sense:** A full circle is 360 degrees and is the whole 100%.
- **Use it when:** a pie chart gives shares and the question gives a total.

**2. Percentage change**

$$\text{percent change} = \frac{\text{new} - \text{old}}{\text{old}} \times 100$$

- **Read it as:** "change divided by the old value, times 100."
- **Why it makes sense:** The change is compared with where you started.
- **Use it when:** you compare two years or two values. Divide by the **old** value, not the new one.

**3. Weighted mean from a frequency chart**

$$\bar{X} = \frac{\sum f_i x_i}{\sum f_i}$$

- **Read it as:** "X bar equals the sum of f times x, divided by the sum of f."
- **Symbols:** $\bar{X}$ ("X bar") is the mean; $x_i$ is each value; $f_i$ is its frequency; $\sum$ (sigma) means "add them all up".
- **Why it makes sense:** If 6 patients came in 40 shifts, the number 6 appears 40 times in the full list, so it adds $6 \times 40$.
- **Use it when:** a bar chart shows how often each value happened.

**4. Mean through a straight-line formula**

$$Y = a(X - c) \;\Rightarrow\; \bar{Y} = a(\bar{X} - c)$$

- **Read it as:** "if Y is a times (X minus c), then the mean of Y is a times (the mean of X minus c)."
- **Symbols:** $a$ and $c$ are fixed numbers.
- **Why it makes sense:** Subtracting $c$ from every value lowers the average by $c$. Multiplying every value by $a$ multiplies the average by $a$.
- **Use it when:** earnings or scores are given as a formula of a count.

### Where the formulas come from
All four DI formulas come from two basic ideas: "a share is a part of the whole" and "the mean is total divided by count". Here is how each is built.

**A. Value from a pie chart (formula 1)**

Start with a slice of $90^\circ$ in a chart of 2000 students.

1. The whole circle, $360^\circ$, stands for all 2000 students, because a pie chart draws the whole as a full circle.
2. The slice is $\frac{90}{360} = \frac14$ of the circle, because a slice's share is its angle divided by the full angle.
3. As a percent, $\frac14 = 25\%$, by multiplying by 100.
4. The value is $25\%$ of 2000 $= \frac{25}{100} \times 2000 = 500$ students, by the meaning of percent.

In symbols: share $= \frac{\theta}{360^\circ} \times 100\%$, and value $= \frac{\text{share}}{100} \times \text{base}$. The word "correct" in "correct base" matters: the circle stands for whatever total the chart says (for example "valid votes"), so the percent must be taken of that total.

**B. Percentage change (formula 2)**

1. Suppose sales go from 50 to 60. The change is $60 - 50 = 10$.
2. We ask "10 is what part of where we started?" So we divide by the starting value: $\frac{10}{50} = 0.2$. We use the old value because a change is measured from the starting point.
3. Multiply by 100 to write it as a percent: $20\%$.

This is formula 1 of Quantitative Aptitude read backwards. There, new $= \text{old} \times (1 + \frac{x}{100})$. Solve for $x$: divide by old, subtract 1, multiply by 100, and you get $x = \frac{\text{new} - \text{old}}{\text{old}} \times 100$.

**C. Weighted mean (formula 3)**

Start with numbers: the values 1, 2, 3 occur 2, 5 and 3 times.

1. Write out the full list: 1, 1, 2, 2, 2, 2, 2, 3, 3, 3. This is what the frequencies mean.
2. The plain mean of this list is $\frac{\text{sum}}{\text{count}}$, by the definition of mean.
3. The sum: the 1s give $1 \times 2$, the 2s give $2 \times 5$, the 3s give $3 \times 3$. Adding: $2 + 10 + 9 = 21$. We multiply because adding the same number $f$ times is the same as multiplying by $f$.
4. The count is $2 + 5 + 3 = 10$, the total of the frequencies.
5. Mean $= \frac{21}{10} = 2.1$.

In symbols: the sum is $\sum f_i x_i$ (step 3) and the count is $\sum f_i$ (step 4). So

$$\bar{X} = \frac{\sum f_i x_i}{\sum f_i}$$

The weighted mean is not a new kind of average. It is the plain mean of the full list, written in a shorter way.

**D. Mean through a straight-line formula (formula 4)**

Start with numbers: patients on 3 days are 4, 5 and 9, and earnings are $Y = 100(X - 1)$.

1. Earnings each day: $100 \times 3 = 300$, $100 \times 4 = 400$, $100 \times 8 = 800$. Mean earning $= \frac{1500}{3} = 500$.
2. Mean patients $\bar{X} = \frac{4 + 5 + 9}{3} = 6$. Put it in the formula: $100(6 - 1) = 500$. Same answer.

Now in symbols, with $n$ values $X_1, \ldots, X_n$.

1. $\bar{Y} = \frac{1}{n} \sum a(X_i - c)$, by the definition of mean.
2. Take $a$ outside the sum, because every term has the same factor $a$: $\bar{Y} = \frac{a}{n} \sum (X_i - c)$.
3. Split the sum: $\sum (X_i - c) = \sum X_i - nc$, because $c$ is subtracted $n$ times.
4. So $\bar{Y} = a\left(\frac{\sum X_i}{n} - c\right) = a(\bar{X} - c)$.

This works only for straight-line rules. For a curved rule like $Y = X^2$, the mean of $Y$ is not $\bar{X}^2$, so do not use the shortcut there.

### Examples
**Example 1 (easy):** In a class test, the marks are 12, 15, 9, 15, 14. How many students scored at least 14?

1. "At least 14" means 14 or more, so 14 itself counts.
2. The marks that qualify are 15, 15 and 14.

**Result:** 3 students.

**Example 2 (medium):** 60,000 votes were cast and 4,000 were invalid. Candidate X got 30% of the valid votes. How many votes did X get?

1. Valid votes $= 60000 - 4000 = 56000$.
2. $0.30 \times 56000 = 16800$.

**Result:** 16,800. The trap answer is $0.30 \times 60000 = 18000$.

**Example 3 (GATE level):** Over 50 days, a shop got 2 orders on 10 days, 3 orders on 25 days and 4 orders on 15 days. Profit per day is $200(X - 1)$ rupees, where $X$ is the number of orders. Find the mean daily profit.

1. Total days $= 10 + 25 + 15 = 50$.
2. $\bar{X} = \frac{2(10) + 3(25) + 4(15)}{50} = \frac{20 + 75 + 60}{50} = \frac{155}{50} = 3.1$.
3. Mean profit $= 200(3.1 - 1) = 200 \times 2.1 = 420$.

**Result:** 420 rupees. (Check the slow way: days with 2 orders earn 200, with 3 earn 400, with 4 earn 600. Mean $= \frac{200(10) + 400(25) + 600(15)}{50} = \frac{21000}{50} = 420$.)

### Common mistakes
> **Common mistake:** Taking the percentage of the wrong base, like total votes instead of valid votes (2024 Q.5).

> **Common mistake:** Treating "$\le 4$" as "$< 4$" and leaving out the 4s. GATE puts the wrong count among the options (2025 Q.3).

> **Common mistake:** Forgetting the fixed part of a formula. In $1000(X - 0.2)$, using $1000\bar{X}$ alone gives a trap option (2025 Q.10).

> **Common mistake:** Losing count in a big grid. Count row by row, write each row's total, and add at the end.

> **Common mistake:** Dividing by the new value in a percentage change. Always divide by the old (starting) value.

### How GATE asks it
- **2024 Q.5:** pie chart of valid votes; B and C share 45% of 1,10,000 valid votes, which is 49,500. The trap used 1,15,000.
- **2025 Q.3:** count pixels with $U \le 4$ in a 4 by 4 grid: $4 + 3 + 2 + 2 = 11$. The trap (8) left out the 4s.
- **2025 Q.10 (2 marks):** weighted mean from a bar chart ($\bar{X} = 6.3$), then earnings $1000(6.3 - 0.2) = 6100$.

> **GATE tip:** The wrong options are built from common mistakes. If your answer matches an option, quickly check that you did not use the wrong base or the wrong inequality.

### Summary
- First, every chart stands for some whole, so we find the correct base before taking any percent.
- A pie slice's share is its angle over 360, and a percent change compares the change with the starting value.
- A frequency chart is a short way of writing a long list, so the weighted mean is just the plain mean of that list: $\frac{\sum f_i x_i}{\sum f_i}$.
- Because a mean is a sum divided by a count, it passes straight through a straight-line rule: $\bar{Y} = a(\bar{X} - c)$.
- So most DI errors are reading errors: the wrong base, or leaving out the boundary value in "at most" and "at least".
- Next topic builds on this: in ML, the weighted mean becomes the expected value $E[X]$, and the straight-line rule becomes linearity of expectation.

### Quick self-check
1. A pie slice has angle 90 degrees. What percent of the total is it?
**Answer:** $\frac{90}{360} \times 100 = 25\%$.

2. The values 1, 2, 3 occur 2, 5 and 3 times. What is the mean?
**Answer:** $\frac{1(2) + 2(5) + 3(3)}{10} = \frac{2 + 10 + 9}{10} = 2.1$.

3. A price goes from 50 to 60. What is the percent change?
**Answer:** $\frac{60 - 50}{50} \times 100 = 20\%$.
